UNIT 04 · LESSON 01

Transactions, ACID & transaction states

IN PLAIN WORDS

A transaction is one complete job, such as moving money between accounts. ACID describes its promises; logs and recovery help make a committed job durable and an incomplete job atomic.

By the end, you can:
  • Define transactions and explain the ACID promises
  • Read active, committed, failed, and aborted states
  • Explain how logs help implement atomicity and durability

EXPLANATION
TOPIC 01

Transaction

DEFINITION

A transaction is a sequence of database operations treated as one logical unit of work.

A transaction may read or write several values. The DBMS should not leave only half of the intended result.

COMMIT makes successful changes final. ROLLBACK cancels a failed transaction's changes.

QUICK EXAMPLE

A transfer from account A to B is one transaction even though it updates two rows.

TOPIC 02

The four ACID promises

DEFINITION

ACID stands for Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, and Durability.

Atomicity: all operations happen or none do. Consistency: a correct transaction preserves declared rules. Isolation: concurrent transactions do not produce an invalid combined result.

Durability: once committed, a result survives a crash. Logging and recovery support atomicity and durability; concurrency control supports isolation.

QUICK EXAMPLE

If a transfer debits A and credits B, A+B should remain the same. A crash after only the debit must not leave money missing.

TOPIC 03

Transaction states

DEFINITION

A transaction moves from active work toward commit, or from failure toward abort.

A common state diagram includes Active, Partially Committed, Committed, Failed, Aborted, and Terminated.

After the last statement, the transaction is partially committed while final checks and log actions complete. It may then commit, or a failure may move it to Failed and then Aborted.

QUICK EXAMPLE

A transaction that cannot satisfy a constraint may fail before commit and be rolled back.

TOPIC 04

Implementation of atomicity and durability

DEFINITION

Atomicity means a transaction takes effect completely or not at all. Durability means a committed result survives a crash.

A common method uses a log. Before a changed data page is written, its log record is made stable (write-ahead logging). The log stores enough information to undo an unfinished change or redo committed work.

After a crash, recovery undoes transactions that did not commit and redoes committed updates that were not copied to their data pages. The system confirms commit only after its required durable log record is safe.

Some systems also teach shadow paging: write changed pages separately and switch the database root pointer at commit. This is an alternative idea; logging is common in database systems.

QUICK EXAMPLE

A transfer moves 20 from A to B. If the machine crashes after subtracting 20 from A but before adding it to B, recovery undoes the debit unless the transaction had committed. If commit was recorded but a page was not flushed, recovery redoes the missing update.

WORKED EXAMPLE · SELECT A STEP

Move 10 points between two accounts

Account A has 50 points and account B has 20. Move 10 points from A to B.

STEP 01

Read both accounts

The transaction checks that A has 50 and B has 20.

STEP 02

Make both changes

Subtract 10 from A and add 10 to B. If one change fails, undo the other so points are not lost.

STEP 03

Save the completed transfer

When both changes are correct, the transaction commits. A later crash must not erase this saved transfer.

RESULT

Atomicity means both changes happen or neither happens. Durability means a committed change stays saved. The total remains 70.
